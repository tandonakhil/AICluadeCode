1 · Your all-in-the-hamburger decision: what it costs, and what I did about it

You were offered keeping Queue and Approvals persistent and chose everything in the drawer. That stands. Here is the honest accounting, because you asked whether it creates a problem I can design around or one I cannot.

What it costs — three things, named

1. The nav badges go dark. UX_KB §4 made badges the volume control expressed in the chrome: a reviewer who sees “3” before clicking is budgeting attention correctly. Behind a drawer, nobody sees them.

2. The return path gets longer. Six items a night × (open drawer → find queue → click) is the tax, and it is paid at exactly the moment — item four, 11pm — when attention is already spent.

3. Refusals loses its standing rebuke. §5.11 keeps What we refuse to do in global nav on the argument that burying a refusal makes it look like an omission. A drawer is closer to a settings drawer than the old sidebar was.

Designed around — conditionally, and the condition is not mine to meet

Cost 1 is fully answered. Every badge’s number is on this page, larger, with a destination and a qualifier. While you are on home, chrome badges were always redundant.

Cost 2 is answered by one control, not by nav. The top bar keeps “Close — 2026-06 · 4 left”: one control, to one screen, carrying the one badge that matters mid-flow. That is not navigation smuggled back in — it goes to exactly the screen you asked to carry the work.

Cost 3 is answered by content, not position. The refusal block is on all three home pages, in the product’s serif voice, at the moment a user is most likely to want the thing it refuses. That is a stronger placement than a nav row nobody clicked.

The one problem I cannot fully design around, stated plainly

The return path only works if the post-resolution step exists, and it does not. UX_KB A2.6 lists it as an open severity-A gap: J1’s final step produces no confirmation, no queue removal, no badge change and no next-item. With a persistent sidebar that was an irritation — you could always click “My queue” again. With everything in a drawer it becomes the dominant interaction of the night: save an item, land nowhere, open the drawer, find the queue, and count which rows you have already done because nothing removed them.

So: designable around, conditional on one build item. After a resolution is recorded the product must land the user on the next routed item, or back on this page with the count decremented. That is code-agent’s smallest listed fix and it is now load-bearing rather than a nicety. If it is not built, your decision costs more than the clutter it removes, and I would say so rather than ship the drawer over a dead end. I am not asking you to reverse the decision — I am asking that this one item ship with it.

2 · The close tracker

Drawn from the declared close calendar that already exists in the build — close.pilot_2026_06@1, five checkpoints at 08:00 UTC on close days 1–5, and AC-F26-05/AC-F38-11’s arithmetic against them. Nothing here is invented. One correction to the existing prototype: v2-close.html says “day 3 of 8”. The declared calendar has five checkpoints, so the honest figure is day 3 of 5. A tracker that disagrees with the calendar it is computed from is worse than no tracker.

The graphic is the gap, not the progress

A passed checkpoint is filled neutral ink — never green, because a cut-off that has gone by is a fact, not an achievement, and “fine, move on” is the affect a depleted reviewer must not be handed. What renders in severity colour is the bracket between the checkpoint the refresh was due by (day 2) and the one it landed in (day 3). The staleness sentence is that bracket’s label, at 15px semibold rust, not a second assertion beside it.

Four states, because the fourth is the honest one

On calendar · refreshed after the last checkpoint · no refresh observed (which is the absence of an answer, not “zero days behind”) · no calendar declared, where the tracker renders as an absence in the product’s serif voice rather than as five grey stops. Five grey stops would look like a close that has not started — a claim nobody made. AC-F38-11 is currently unmet for that path (register 6) and the block says so.

3 · Home — staff accountant (A. Reyes)

The person the product harms first and worst if it is wrong, and the one whose whole night now runs through this page. Six items are routed; the page names all six and points each at an object.

The abstention is pulled out of the count, not folded into it

The queue badge is 6. The page says “five findings and one the system could not settle” and gives the abstention its own dashed-slate row in serif. A home page that said “6 findings” would have quietly converted an abstention into a finding in the one place a tired reader takes the number at face value. The severity ramp draws the five and then a gap, then the dashed abstention swatch outside it.

The routing cap is a qualifier, not a tile

Tonight: 6 routed against a cap of 12. It does not bind, so it implies no act and does not earn a tile — it renders as a 13px qualifier under the queue tile. On a night when it does bind, it becomes an action item on the controller’s home, because raising a cap is a controller’s recorded control event and never a reviewer’s. Same fact, two homes, decided by whether anyone can act on it.
home-staff.html · open full size · dark

A build consequence that only appears once counts are on a home page

Queue counts include probes; coverage figures exclude them. The build is already right about this — state.conclusion excludes probes from the coverage population under AC-F12-16, because a coverage statement is a claim about the customer’s ledger and a probe is one we manufactured, while nav_badges["queue"] counts every admitted item because a probe must be indistinguishable. Putting both numbers on one page for the first time makes it possible to “tidy” them into agreement. They must not agree. A “5 findings” figure computed by subtracting the probe would make the probe detectable by arithmetic, which is the one thing AC-F41-08 forbids.

4 · Home — controller (D. Okafor)

Six action items, every one drawn from a fact the build already computes, and a panel naming the four real figures that are deliberately not here.

The strongest row is one nobody would have specified

“An agent acting tonight is not ready.” The pilot’s readiness_report for crossperiod-surveillance has P2 unmet — one F32 forward disposition missed in 2026-05 and not yet explained. That is the same miss sitting on A. Reyes’ home page tonight. The two personas’ pages are looking at one fact from two ends, and neither had a surface where it was visible. This is real, computed, and it is the row I would defend hardest.

An explicit zero that is an action item

“0 routing caps raised tonight — 1 reviewer routed: A. Reyes, 6 against a cap of 12.” A zero with its denominator, never a blank and never omitted. It qualifies as an action item because a raise is the only way a reviewer receives more than their cap and it is a control event the controller owns — so “none happened” is a thing they must be able to see they have confirmed, and a night when it is not zero is a night that needs them.

5 · Home — FP&A analyst

You asked what the honest content is for a persona whose Inquire surface does not exist. Here it is, and it is the page I most want you to look at.

Two facts you should hold before reading it

1. There is no FP&A persona in the build. state.PERSONAS carries exactly two — staff accountant and controller. A third is net-new scope requiring a ruling, not a design decision (UX_KB A2.3 / A2.6, severity A). The page below is rendered against a persona that does not yet exist.
2. Nothing in this product routes to an FP&A analyst. So the temptation is to fill the page with coverage, detections and dwell — figures that are real and imply no act. I refused.

The design: apply the abstention grammar to a whole persona

The headline is “Nothing is routed to you, and that is a fact about this product, not about this close.” Then the three things that are genuinely obtainable from 2026-06 today: the 7 accounts with a quotable decomposition (stamped with certified query, version and dataset version — the stamp is what makes it defensible in a variance meeting), the 3 accounts nothing has scanned (the figure most worth knowing before the meeting), and the 1 abstention. That is a genuinely useful page and every row is true.

Refused and deferred, adjacent, so the contrast teaches

Refused (solid slate, filled): A22 — we will not write the sentence that explains why the account moved. Deferred (dashed outline, no fill): Inquire mode and an FP&A identity — planned, not refused. Side by side, because a user who only ever sees one treatment cannot learn to tell them apart, and the distinction is carried in the wording too, so it survives greyscale and a screen reader with all CSS off.

7 · Which figures earned a place, and which did not

Your instruction was KPIs framed as that user’s action items, and a number that implies no action does not belong here. I made that structural rather than editorial.

The rule, and how the component enforces it

A figure earns a place only if it has all three of: (a) a number, (b) a reliability qualifier at ≥13px, (c) a destination that is a specific object — not a section. The action tile .act is an <a>, and there is no variant without a .q qualifier. A tile with no destination and a tile with no qualifier cannot be expressed in the component at all, which is the same move §5.3 makes for the conclusion string: unreachable, not validated out.
